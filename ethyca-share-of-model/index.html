01 — THE ASTRALIS GAP

The engines file Ethyca under the category it is leaving, not the one it is entering

An engine builds a shortlist from what it has read about you. What it has read about Ethyca is privacy-operations content — consent, data subject requests, data mapping. So when a question sounds like privacy operations, Ethyca surfaces. When a question sounds like governing what AI agents may do with enterprise data, it doesn't. That is a content and positioning gap, not a product gap: the same engines describe Astralis accurately the moment you say its name.

04 — EVERY QUESTION

The more the buyer sounds like an engineer, the fewer vendors anyone gets named

How a buyer phrases the question decides whether they get a shopping list or a lecture. Name the category — "best AI data governance platforms" — and all four engines hand back a tidy vendor table, averaging 8.5 names. Describe the problem instead — "stop our internal assistant surfacing personal data someone shouldn't see" — and the engines switch into architecture mode: identity propagation, filtering before retrieval, least privilege. Useful advice, no vendors.

That matters for Ethyca specifically, because the buyer for Astralis is the engineering and security side, and that buyer is the one most likely to describe a problem rather than name a category. Of the 28 answers to problem-described questions, 12 named no vendor at all — ChatGPT in 4 of its 16 answers, Perplexity in 6. Those are answers no brand could have won. Strip them out and the honest denominator is 51 answers where a shortlist existed; Ethyca was on 11 of them, and absent from 40.

05 — WHO WRITES THE SHORTLIST

Engines read pages, then recommend whoever those pages talk about — so whoever wrote the pages writes the shortlist

None of these engines holds an opinion about privacy software. Each one searches, reads a handful of pages, and summarises them. So the recommendation is decided upstream, by which pages get read. That makes the reading list the most actionable thing in this report, because it is the part Ethyca can change directly.

Here is the striking result. Across the 64 answers, ethyca.com is the most-read vendor site in the category — cited in 8 answers, ahead of onetrust.com on 5 and securiti.ai and transcend.io on 4. Ethyca is not being ignored by the engines. It is being read more than anyone, and converting that into a recommendation 5 times out of 8.

THE ONE TO FIX THIS WEEK

Ethyca's own comparison page is being used to build the shortlist that replaces Ethyca

ethyca.com/guides/onetrust-alternatives is the single most-cited Ethyca page — read in 3 of the 4 answers to "best alternatives to OneTrust", and again when engines were asked which companies are most similar to Ethyca.

It works as intended on Claude and Perplexity: both read it and put Ethyca on the list. On ChatGPT it does the opposite. ChatGPT read that page, then recommended Transcend, Securiti, BigID and Credo AI — and did not name Ethyca once. Asked separately which companies are most like Ethyca, ChatGPT cited the same page and answered "Transcend is the closest overall match."

A page that lists competitors generously and positions Ethyca lightly reads, to an engine, as a page about those competitors.

Which Ethyca pages the engines actually read

Every one of the 8 category-question citations was a /guides/ or /news/ page: the OneTrust alternatives guide, three consent-management pieces, an AI-governance guide, a data-subject-request architecture guide and a data-mapping explainer.

The product pages — /astralis, /for-enterprise, /fides — were read only when a question named Ethyca. Guides get you into the answer. Product pages are read after someone already knows your name.

And the third-party pages deciding the rest

After ethyca.com, the most-read sources are not analysts or review sites. They are content-marketing listicles published by small vendors and adjacent tools: ovaledge.com (7 answers), secureprivacy.ai (6), netwrix.com (5), cloudeagle.ai (5), then GitHub and arXiv. G2 was cited in 3. The pages shaping this category's shortlists are mostly written by companies small enough that Ethyca can out-publish them.

Five months after launch, nobody is searching for Astralis

Branded search is the cheapest read on brand strength there is: it counts people who went looking for you by name, which is the behaviour brand building is supposed to produce. We measured all 19 brands the same way — the brand name plus a fixed set of 14 buying-intent phrases, so the comparison is like for like.

Of that 510, nearly all is the bare word "ethyca". Every commercial phrase we tested — pricing, reviews, alternatives, demo, competitors — returned zero volume. People look up the name; they don't shop the brand.

Astralis, as a product anyone searches for.

"ethyca astralis", "astralis ai governance", "astralis data governance", "astralis policy enforcement", "astralis runtime governance" — all zero. We re-checked every zero in the study on a second pull to rule out a reporting gap; they are real.

The bare word "astralis" does get 1,900 searches a month. It belongs to a Danish esports team: "astralis cs2" (720), "astralis counter strike" (140), "astralis roster" (50). The name is already owned, and not by Ethyca.

One more thing search can tell us that the engines can't. Ethyca's search interest is too small for Google Trends to report in most weeks — it registered in 4 of 262 weeks over five years, and 4 of the last 53. Over that same five years OneTrust, Securiti, BigID and TrustArc each grew their interest by 60–100%. We are deliberately not drawing a trend line for Ethyca: with 4 readable weeks in five years, there is nothing to trend. The honest statement is that Ethyca is below the floor of what Google will report, while the category phrase "ai data governance" registers in all 53 weeks of the past year and is one of the highest-interest terms we measured. The demand exists. It has not attached to this brand.

07 — WHAT AI SAYS WHEN YOU NAME THEM

There is no reputation problem to fix. The story lands perfectly — it just has to be asked for

We asked all four engines five questions that name Ethyca directly. This part is assessed by reading the answers rather than counting mentions, and it is the good news in this report: the positioning is intact, consistent and flattering on every engine.

Astralis is explained correctly, by all four

Every engine led with purpose-based access control — not just who may access data but what they may use it for — and with enforcement happening at the moment of access rather than in a periodic review. All four connected it to the Fides taxonomy. ChatGPT and Perplexity also picked up the risk-and-regulatory model by name. This is the intended message, repeated back without distortion.

Against OneTrust, Ethyca is framed as the technical choice

All four drew the same line: OneTrust for a broad, committee-run governance programme; Ethyca when the job is enforcing data use inside systems, pipelines and agents. Gemini called it "governance-as-code and runtime enforcement versus enterprise GRC". ChatGPT was careful to note both vendors now advertise runtime controls, so the contrast shouldn't be overstated. Nobody argued Ethyca was the weaker product.

Fides carries real credibility

Asked about Fides, Claude and Perplexity both cited its adoption as the basis of the IAB Tech Lab privacy taxonomy. Gemini described Ethyca's differentiator as "privacy-as-code… enforced programmatically inside the data stack and CI/CD pipelines" — the engineering-led framing, unprompted. The open-source asset is doing more brand work than anything else Ethyca owns.

Two things worth correcting

Claude dates the Astralis launch to 4 August. Separately, when asked to compare Ethyca with OneTrust, Claude reported that only its Ethyca searches succeeded and that its OneTrust detail came from memory — so a comparison a buyer reads may be half-sourced without saying so prominently.

And the answer to "who is most like Ethyca" is, on two engines, Transcend — sourced from Ethyca's own guide.

08 — WHAT THIS CHANGES

Four moves, in the order the evidence supports

1

Rewrite the comparison pages so they argue for Ethyca

The OneTrust alternatives guide is already the most-read page Ethyca has, and on ChatGPT it currently hands the recommendation to Transcend. This is the highest-leverage fix in the report: the distribution is won, only the content is losing. Same treatment for the other comparison pages, and add the ones that don't exist yet — the engines are being asked for alternatives to BigID, Securiti and Collibra too.

2

Publish the AI-governance guides that don't exist, in the buyer's words

Ethyca's guides are read more than any competitor's — but they are about consent, data subject requests and data mapping, which is exactly why the engines file Ethyca under privacy operations. The seven AI-governance questions where Ethyca scored 1 of 28 are answerable with the content Ethyca is uniquely qualified to write: enforcing policy at the point of access, governing what agents may do with production data, personal data in retrieval pipelines. Write those as guides, not as product pages — the engines read guides to build shortlists and product pages only after someone names you.

3

Lead with Fides, not around it

Fides is the only thing all four engines agree Ethyca is for, and the only unanimous result in the study. It is also the proof the engines reach for when explaining why Ethyca is credible. Treat it as the top of the funnel it already is, and make the path from Fides to Astralis explicit in the content the engines are reading.

4

Decide what "Astralis" is for, given nobody will search for it

A product name with zero search volume whose token belongs to an esports team will not become a search asset on any realistic budget. That is fine if Astralis is a name for sales conversations and the brand that carries demand is Ethyca. It is a problem if the plan assumed people would go looking for Astralis. The measurement says: build salience for Ethyca in the AI-governance category, and let Astralis be what the buyer finds when they get there.

One thing we still can't see

Ethyca's Google Search Console isn't connected to the reporting stack, so this report has no first-party view of which queries already surface ethyca.com and which get clicked. That is the one surface that would tell us whether the guides are being found on category terms or only on the brand name — and it is the cheapest gap to close before the next measurement.

09 — METHOD

Exactly what we did

Why measure this instead of running a survey

A brand-recall survey needs a representative panel of your buyer. For enterprise AI data governance that panel doesn't exist at a workable cost — the buyer is a fraction of a percent of any general business audience. Behavioural measurement sidesteps the problem: everyone who searches for a brand, or asks an AI for a recommendation, has selected themselves in. The trade-off is that this sees people who are currently acting, so it measures salience among buyers in motion and under-counts dormant awareness. It is a complement to a thin attitudinal survey, not a replacement.

The questions

16 questions written in the buyer's language, none naming Ethyca, deliberately spread across the journey: drawing up a shortlist, segment fit, integration, the job to be done, and switching away from an incumbent. The set also varies how each question is framed — naming the category, naming the buyer's role, or describing the problem — because that framing turned out to change the answer more than anything else. A further 5 questions name Ethyca directly; those are read qualitatively and are excluded from every count in this report.

Counting

Each of the 16 questions was put to ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Perplexity with live web search enabled — 64 answers. A brand scores once per answer no matter how often it is named, so this measures breadth of recommendation rather than word count. 83 brands were named in total, and the vocabulary was built from the answers themselves rather than from a list we brought with us.

How we checked the numbers

Every answer was tallied twice, by two independently written counters, and compared answer by answer. The first pass disagreed on one of the 64; reading the raw text showed the second counter had been fooled by a model's internal planning notes and by a citation link. Fixed, the two agree on all 64 answers and all 381 mentions. Every brand whose name is also an ordinary English word was then inspected in context by hand. The 13 answers that named no vendor at all were read in full to confirm they were genuinely vendor-free rather than a counting failure.

Branded search

For each of 19 brands, the brand name plus 14 fixed buying-intent phrases, United States, 12-month average. Where a brand name is also a common word the volume is modelled rather than taken at face value: "transcend", "ketch" and "private ai" all collect searches meant for other things, and counting them raw would have handed one vendor 39% of the category. Every keyword that returned zero was re-pulled in a differently composed batch to be sure a zero was a real zero.

What we chose not to claim

We don't report a 12-month momentum trend. Every brand in the set, and the category phrase itself, appeared to fall 35–58% in recent weeks — and the cause is that Google withholds data for weeks below a reporting threshold, which drags the recent average down for exactly the smaller brands we care about. The five-year view is the only defensible read, and for Ethyca even that is too sparse to trend.

This is a baseline

An earlier look in June 2026 used two engines and a different question set, so its numbers are not comparable with these and are not mixed in. Directionally it found the same thing: accurate when named, absent from recommendations, with Fides the one recognised asset. Treat November 2026 as the baseline and re-run the identical 21 questions on the same four engines each quarter. Compare per-engine counts out of 16 and the pooled count out of 64 — never a figure built on a different number of engines.

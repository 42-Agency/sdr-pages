The actual answer to your question

Wizard-level shows up in what it refused to do.

Tool-fluent means you can make the machine produce output. Wizard-level means the machine makes the calls a sharp operator would make — including restraint. Here are the four places this run did that. These are the things I'd grade a take-home on.

01

It threw away the most impressive signal.

330 live ads would normally route straight to an “audit offer” play. The chain discarded it — wrong register for a warm peer note. Tool-fluent dumps every signal it finds. Wizard-level scores each one against the actual goal and drops what doesn't change the move.

02

It knew when not to enrich.

It marked “new marketing leader: false” — you've run marketing here 3+ years, so it refused to fabricate a fake “congrats on the new role” hook. And it excluded your work email because proton.ai blocks verification and it wouldn't guess a pattern that could burn sender reputation. Restraint is the skill most tools don't have.

03

It surfaced uncertainty instead of hiding it.

Your Google Ads account is labeled “PRM Solutions.” The chain flagged it as possibly a third party on the domain rather than asserting it as yours. Wizards caveat what they can't confirm. Tool-jockeys present everything with the same false confidence.

04

It rebuilt the play when the situation didn't fit the template.

The default output is a 4-email cold sequence. It saw a warm, 1st-degree relationship and threw the cold logic out — rewriting the whole angle as a peer demonstration with the artifact as the payload. Judgment over template is the entire ballgame.

For the take-home you're designing

Grade the judgment calls, not the output.

Hand a candidate a real (or fictional) company and a goal — not a tool checklist. Then score the four moves above: Did they throw away the impressive-but-irrelevant signal? Did they refuse to fabricate? Did they flag what they weren't sure of? Did they change the plan when the context broke the template?
